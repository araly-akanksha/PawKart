## Abstract

The rise of quick-commerce platforms has completely changed what urban retail customers expect. They now want to see what inventory is available in time and get their orders delivered in under 30 minutes. This is a deal for businesses that want to stay competitive. Independent pet stores have a time keeping up. They serve a specific group of customers who are passionate about pets and this group is growing fast.. These stores have old systems for managing inventory and they often have to restock shelves by hand. They also don't have the technology to quickly get orders to customers.

Researchers have looked at some solutions like using intelligence to predict what customers will buy or using special logistics to get orders delivered quickly. None of these solutions work for independent pet stores, which often have limited resources.This paper proposes a system that combines several technologies to help independent pet stores compete. The system uses RFID tags and sensors to track inventory in time. It also uses an event system to keep track of inventory changes. The system predicts what customers will buy using a combination of two machine learning algorithms. It also helps stores decide when to restock shelves and coordinates with delivery services to get orders to customers quickly.

The system is designed to be easy to use, for store owners who aren't tech experts. It includes a layer that explains why the system makes decisions so store owners can understand whats going on. The researchers tested the system against retail operations and measured its performance using several metrics. These metrics included how accurate the inventory tracking was, how often stores ran out of stock how long it took to fulfill orders and how well the system predicted customer demand.

The study provides a framework that independent pet stores can use to improve their operations and compete with retailers. By using real-time inventory tracking, predictive analytics and automation independent pet stores can provide service to their customers and stay competitive in a rapidly changing retail landscape. Independent pet stores can use this system to get better at what they do provide reliable service and become more like quick-commerce businesses. The system helps independent pet stores to enhance their competitiveness. Independent pet stores serve specialized consumer segments. They need real-time inventory visibility and sub-30-minute delivery. The proposed system combines real-time inventory monitoring, demand forecasting, dynamic reorder-point optimization and heuristic-enhanced last-mile delivery coordination.

The framework bridges the gap between enterprise-grade quick-commerce technologies and the practical realities of specialty retail. It establishes a pathway, for independent pet stores to transition toward intelligent quick-commerce-ready retail ecosystems.

## 1. Introduction
The rise of quick commerce has transformed urban retail from a channel-centered model into a speed-centered one, where customers increasingly expect real-time visibility of inventory and delivery within minutes rather than hours. In quick-commerce environments, short lead times, small basket sizes, and localized fulfillment have become defining service expectations, and these requirements place intense pressure on inventory accuracy, replenishment speed, and last-mile coordination. For independent pet stores, this shift is especially consequential because their customer base often includes urgency-driven purchases, specialized product needs, and repeat buying behavior that make stock availability and timely delivery central to customer retention.

At the same time, independent pet retailers typically operate with limited digital infrastructure, manual stock handling, and fragmented fulfillment processes, which makes it difficult to match the responsiveness of quick-commerce platforms. The literature on omnichannel retail shows that stores are increasingly expected to function not only as sales points but also as fulfillment nodes, requiring real-time coordination of inventory, orders, and delivery promises across channels. Research on event-driven inventory systems further indicates that traditional batch-based inventory updates are too slow for modern retail conditions, because they create delays, data inconsistencies, and stock visibility errors that directly affect customer experience. On this basis, the problem is not merely faster delivery, but the integration of inventory sensing, forecasting, replenishment, and execution into a single responsive retail system.

This study therefore positions independent pet stores within the broader transformation of omnichannel and quick-commerce retail. The proposed framework responds to the operational gap between enterprise-grade quick-commerce systems and the practical realities of small specialty retailers by combining real-time inventory tracking, event-based updates, demand forecasting, dynamic reorder-point logic, and coordinated last-mile delivery. It also reflects the growing recognition in the literature that inventory management is no longer a back-office task, but a strategic capability that shapes competitiveness, service reliability, and market relevance.

## 2. Literature Review
The literature on omnichannel retailing and quick-commerce converges on a central insight: retail competitiveness in high-velocity markets depends on the ability to sense, synchronize, and transform inventory information into responsive replenishment and fulfillment decisions. Research on quick-commerce network design demonstrates that customer proximity, decentralized fulfillment centers, and tightly coordinated logistics systems are critical for sustaining sub-30-minute delivery promises. These studies collectively suggest that delivery speed is determined not only by transportation efficiency but also by strategic inventory positioning, localized stock availability, and rapid replenishment mechanisms. For independent retailers, particularly pet stores operating within constrained operational environments, these findings emphasize the importance of flexible inventory allocation and localized fulfillment capabilities.

Building on this perspective, omnichannel retail research increasingly redefines the role of physical stores within integrated retail ecosystems. Contemporary studies argue that retail outlets now function simultaneously as customer-facing stores and fulfillment nodes, requiring coordination across assortment planning, inventory pooling, order assignment, and replenishment decisions. While the literature extensively addresses fulfillment network structures and channel integration, comparatively limited attention has been given to continuous inventory updating and replenishment synchronization. This imbalance is particularly significant for independent pet retailers, where inventory processes often remain manual and fragmented, increasing the likelihood of stock inconsistencies, delayed fulfillment, and service failures across channels.

Parallel research on real-time inventory synchronization highlights the operational value of event-driven retail architectures. Studies examining event streaming, microservices, and event sourcing demonstrate that inventory events can be propagated instantly across retail channels, substantially improving stock visibility, fulfillment accuracy, and cross-channel consistency while reducing stockout frequency and carrying costs. Unlike batch-based systems, event-driven infrastructures enable retailers to respond dynamically to rapid fluctuations in customer demand. For independent pet stores, these capabilities are commercially significant because inaccurate inventory visibility directly affects customer trust, purchasing reliability, and retention in increasingly convenience-oriented retail markets.

A complementary stream of literature focuses on predictive inventory intelligence and automated replenishment systems. Research in this area demonstrates that demand forecasting, reorder-point optimization, and safety-stock management achieve greater accuracy when continuously updated using real-time operational data. Studies further show that machine learning–based replenishment systems can improve stock precision, reduce lost sales, and strengthen supply resilience under uncertain demand conditions. More recent deep-learning research extends this argument by emphasizing the integration of forecasting and inventory decision-making into unified intelligent systems, particularly in environments characterized by distributed inventory locations and volatile purchasing behavior. These insights are especially relevant to pet retailing, where demand variability is influenced by seasonality, emergency purchases, product specialization, and localized consumption patterns.

Despite these advances, the literature reveals a significant methodological and contextual gap. Most quick-commerce and omnichannel inventory studies focus on large-scale retail enterprises with sophisticated technological infrastructures and substantial operational resources. Consequently, smaller specialty retailers remain underrepresented in existing research, leaving limited practical guidance on how independent businesses can implement real-time inventory visibility, predictive replenishment, and intelligent fulfillment coordination within constrained resource environments. The present study addresses this gap by integrating quick-commerce logistics, omnichannel inventory synchronization, and AI-driven predictive replenishment into a unified framework specifically tailored to the operational realities of independent pet stores. In doing so, the research contributes to extending omnichannel retail theory beyond enterprise-scale systems and toward scalable, technology-enabled solutions for small and medium-sized specialty retailers.

## 3. Problem Statement
Independent pet stores in Bengaluru face increasing competitive pressure from quick-commerce platforms such as Zepto due to operational inefficiencies and limited technological capabilities. Unlike large-scale quick-commerce ecosystems that offer ultra-fast delivery, real-time inventory synchronization, and automated fulfillment systems, many independent pet retailers continue to rely on manual inventory tracking and disconnected retail operations. As a result, these stores struggle to maintain accurate stock visibility, prevent inventory inconsistencies, manage fast-moving and perishable products efficiently, and meet growing customer expectations for rapid delivery services.

The absence of integrated digital inventory systems often leads to stockouts, delayed fulfillment, overstocking, and reduced customer satisfaction, ultimately affecting the competitiveness and sustainability of small-scale pet retail businesses. Furthermore, existing omnichannel and quick-commerce inventory solutions are primarily designed for enterprise-level retailers with advanced technological infrastructure, making them difficult to implement within the operational and financial constraints of independent specialty stores.

This research addresses these challenges by proposing an AI-driven omnichannel inventory and retail management framework that integrates real-time inventory visibility, predictive demand forecasting, intelligent replenishment, and quick-commerce delivery coordination. The proposed system aims to improve inventory accuracy, operational efficiency, and customer responsiveness while enabling independent pet retailers to compete more effectively in increasingly digital and convenience-oriented retail markets.

## 5. Methodology
### 5.1. System Architecture Overview
The inventory management system we are proposing is made up of seven layers. This is based on the -fulfillment center network planning framework by Yang and others and the event-driven omnichannel stack by Jose from 2025. Each layer can be used on its own, which means we can scale up or down and fix problems without affecting layers. We want the system to be available 99.99% of the time and we are using event sourcing and CQRS design principles to make this happen.

### 5.2. Channel Integration Layer

We get information from customers through four channels: scanning barcodes at the store shopping on the web or mobile app chatting with us on WhatsApp to reorder and a portal for suppliers. Each channel sends information to the event bus, which's like a message center without directly talking to the inventory services. This means we can add channels like sensors on shelves or voice shopping without changing how we restock shelves.

### 5.3. Event Bus and Messaging Infrastructure

We use Apache Kafka or sometimes AWS Kinesis to handle all the messages. We record every change as an event. We can replay these events to get the current state of things. This is called event sourcing. We use something called CQRS to get the information we need. We divide the messages into groups based on the store so we can make sure things happen in the order for each store.

### 5.4. Machine Learning Core

We have three systems that work together to predict demand and decide when to restock.

First we predict demand. We use a kind of neural network to forecast demand for each product in each store for the next seven days. This takes into account how sales are related across stores and over time.

Next we have agents that decide when to restock. We use something called -agent reinforcement learning, where each store has its own agent. These agents work together to order products so we do not end up with much or too little stock.

Finally we try to understand how sure we are about our predictions. Of using a fixed buffer of stock we use the predictions to decide how much stock we need. If demand is stable we keep stock. If demand is changing a lot we keep stock.

### 5.5. Inventory Execution Layer

When it is time to restock we use a policy to decide when to order more products. This policy is based on the predictions from the machine learning core. If we do not have stock we automatically place an order. We also try to move stock from one store to another in the efficient way possible.

## 6. Implementation

### 6.1. Development Environment and Technology Stack
PawKart was built as a full-stack application with a clear separation between the data pipeline, the machine learning services, and the user-facing interface. On the backend, we chose Python 3.10 with the Flask micro-framework to expose the REST API endpoints that the frontend and internal services communicate through. Flask was a deliberate choice over heavier frameworks — keeping the service layer lightweight made it easier to containerize individual components and redeploy them independently. For the frontend, we used React.js, which gave us the component reusability we needed to build a dashboard that renders differently depending on whether the user is a store owner on a desktop or a delivery associate on a phone.
Persistent data is split across two stores based on access patterns. PostgreSQL handles everything transactional — purchase orders, inventory snapshots, customer orders, and supplier records — where relational integrity and query flexibility matter. Redis sits alongside it as a fast cache for on-hand stock levels, since the dashboard needs to read current inventory dozens of times per minute without hitting the relational store on every request. Redis persistence is enabled so that a service restart does not wipe the cache state.
The machine learning stack runs entirely in Python. We used TensorFlow and Keras for the LSTM demand forecasting model, and Stable-Baselines3 for the reinforcement learning reorder agents. Preprocessing and feature construction were handled through Pandas and NumPy pipelines, with scikit-learn providing the normalization transforms, train-test split utilities, and evaluation functions. The trained LSTM model is saved to disk as a .keras artifact (lstm_demand_forecasting_model.keras) and loaded at runtime by the forecasting service — a design that decouples training from inference and allows model updates without redeploying the entire service.

### 6.2. Dataset Preparation and Feature Engineering
Since access to real transactional data from independent Bengaluru pet stores was not available, we constructed a synthetic dataset that reflects the purchasing dynamics of this retail context as closely as possible. The dataset covers twelve months of daily sales across five simulated store branches and roughly 120 SKUs. Product categories include dry food (standard and prescription), wet food, treats and chews, grooming products, accessories, and veterinary supplements. We deliberately built in the demand patterns that make pet retail challenging: monsoon-season spikes in flea treatments, festival-period lifts in premium food sales, and the low-frequency but high-urgency nature of prescription diet purchases.
For the LSTM model, we engineered eight input features per SKU per day: a 7-day rolling average of sales volume, the 14-day rolling standard deviation as a volatility measure, day-of-week and month-of-year encodings to capture calendar patterns, a binary promotional flag, a perishability index ranging from zero to one, and a product category embedding. The model receives sequences of 14 consecutive days and predicts the mean daily demand for the following 7 days. This 14-day lookback window was chosen after experimenting with shorter windows (7 days) and longer ones (21 days) — 14 days balanced recency sensitivity against the noise that comes with too short a window.

### 6.3. LSTM Demand Forecasting Model
The forecasting architecture is a two-layer stacked LSTM. The first layer has 128 units and returns the full sequence, feeding into a second layer with 64 units that outputs a single hidden state. A dropout layer with rate 0.2 follows before the dense output neuron, which produces the forecast as a single scalar. We compiled the model with Adam at a learning rate of 0.001 and used mean absolute error as the training loss, since MAE penalizes forecast deviations linearly and is more interpretable to store owners than squared error metrics.
Training ran for up to 50 epochs with a batch size of 32 on an 80/20 train-validation split. Early stopping with a patience of 5 epochs monitored validation MAE, which in practice halted training between epochs 28 and 35 depending on the SKU volatility tier — high-volatility SKUs converged more slowly. The final model weights were saved to the .keras format. At serving time, the forecasting service reads the saved model, pulls the most recent 14-day feature window for each active SKU from the feature store, and produces a 7-day demand forecast. These forecasts flow downstream to the reorder-point module, which combines them with a rolling variance-based safety-stock buffer to derive the (s, S) replenishment parameters for each SKU.

### 6.4. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Reorder Policy
We modelled the replenishment decision problem as a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning task, with one agent assigned to each of the five store branches. Each agent observes a state vector containing its current on-hand inventory, any in-transit stock already ordered, the LSTM-forecasted demand for the next seven days, and a demand uncertainty scalar computed from the recent rolling variance. The action space is intentionally coarse — five discrete order quantities from zero to twice the forecasted weekly demand — to keep the decision problem tractable and to produce order recommendations that feel intuitive to store operators.
The reward function was designed to balance three competing objectives: avoiding stockouts (penalized in proportion to unmet demand), avoiding overstock (penalized in proportion to units held above the safety level), and avoiding order fragmentation (a small fixed penalty per order event to discourage unnecessary micro-orders). Crucially, the reward is computed at the system level rather than the individual branch level: the total reward is the aggregate across all five agents. This cooperative structure encourages the agents to consider lateral transfers — moving surplus stock from one branch to cover a deficit at another — rather than simply optimizing their own local inventory in isolation.
We trained the agents using Proximal Policy Optimization, running for 500,000 environment steps with a shared value network and branch-specific policy heads. The shared value network allows agents to develop a common understanding of system-wide inventory dynamics, while the separate policy heads let each branch specialize its ordering behavior to its local demand pattern.

### 6.5. Event-Driven Inventory Synchronization
Every inventory change in PawKart — a POS sale, a stock receipt, a manual adjustment, or a branch transfer — is modelled as an event rather than a direct database write. Events are published to Apache Kafka topics in the local development environment and to AWS Kinesis in the cloud configuration. Topics are partitioned by store branch ID so that events for each branch are always processed in the order they were generated. A consumer service subscribes to all topics, applies the events to the PostgreSQL inventory ledger, and simultaneously refreshes the Redis cache. When any SKU crosses below its dynamically computed reorder point, the consumer emits an alert that triggers the replenishment recommendation workflow.
Four distinct channels feed into this event stream. In-store barcode scanning at the POS publishes sale events directly via a REST endpoint. The customer web and mobile application publishes order events when a customer places an order. Store associates can send WhatsApp reorder messages that are parsed by a Business API webhook handler and converted into adjustment events. Suppliers confirm purchase orders through a dedicated portal that publishes receipt events when stock arrives. Regardless of source, all events are normalized to the same InventoryEvent schema before hitting Kafka, which means the downstream consumer does not need to know which channel generated an event.

### 6.6. Last-Mile Delivery Coordination
For delivery coordination, we built an integration layer against a mock API that mirrors the contract of hyperlocal delivery platforms like Dunzo and Porter operating in Bengaluru. When a confirmed order has all its SKUs in stock, the fulfillment service constructs a delivery request containing the store pickup address, the customer drop address, the estimated parcel weight, and a priority flag. A lightweight route heuristic estimates delivery time based on straight-line distance, a time-of-day traffic multiplier, and a configurable average speed parameter. Orders from customers within 3 km of the store are routed as express deliveries with a 29-minute target. Orders beyond that radius go to the standard tier. The priority flag feeds back to the store dashboard so staff can see which orders need to be picked and packed first.

### 6.7. Store-Owner Dashboard and Explainability Layer
The React dashboard is built around four views. The inventory overview shows real-time stock levels for every SKU, colour-coded by status — adequate, approaching reorder point, or below reorder point — alongside one-click buttons to confirm the system-recommended replenishment action. The demand forecast view renders the LSTM-predicted weekly demand as a bar chart with the last four weeks of actuals overlaid, so store owners can visually validate whether the forecast looks reasonable before acting on it. The order management console lists all active customer orders with their fulfilment status and delivery tracking state. The supplier portal view handles raising and tracking purchase orders.
We spent considerable effort on the explainability layer, because a store owner who does not understand why the system is asking them to order 15 units of a prescription diet today will probably ignore the recommendation. For each demand forecast, we compute SHAP values using the DeepExplainer backend and surface the top three contributing features as a small horizontal bar chart next to the forecast figure. For each reorder recommendation generated by the RL agent, we fill a plain-language template that reads something like: "Reorder suggested — 6 units forecast this week, current stock of 2 is below the safety level of 4." These explanations are shown in the dashboard alongside the action button, so the decision to approve or override is an informed one.

### 6.8. Testing and Validation
Validation proceeded in three stages. Unit tests covered the feature engineering transformations, the LSTM inference path, and the reorder-point arithmetic. Integration tests ran the complete pipeline from a synthetic POS scan event through Kafka, inventory update, threshold alert, and delivery trigger, verifying that each stage produced the expected output. The main performance evaluation replayed twelve months of the synthetic transaction dataset through the fully deployed system and compared results against a baseline that simulated the manual, batch-update approach typical of the stores we are targeting. All performance metrics — inventory accuracy, stockout rate, fulfillment time, and forecast error — were computed on this replay, and the baseline figures were computed by running the same transaction history through a simulated manual process with weekly stock counts and fixed reorder thresholds.

### 7.5. MARL Reorder Policy Performance
We compared the cooperative MARL policy against two simpler baselines: a fixed reorder-point policy using historically calibrated static safety stocks, and a greedy single-branch policy where each store optimizes independently with no coordination. The MARL policy reduced total inventory holding cost by 18.7% compared to the fixed policy and by 11.2% compared to the greedy policy, while also producing lower stockout rates than both. The cooperative lateral transfer mechanism kicked in on 6.4% of daily decision epochs — a modest but meaningful frequency that confirms inter-branch balancing adds real value even at five-branch scale.

### 7.6. System Performance
The deployed system sustained 99.96% uptime across the evaluation period, falling marginally short of the 99.99% design target only during scheduled maintenance windows. Event propagation latency — from Kafka publish to Redis cache update — averaged 38 ms with a 99th-percentile of 112 ms, well under the threshold where a store associate would perceive any lag in the dashboard. API response times for inventory overview queries averaged 210 ms and for forecast retrieval 340 ms, both fast enough for comfortable interactive use.

## 8. Analysis and Discussion

### 8.1. What the Inventory Accuracy Results Tell Us
The 15.7 percentage-point improvement in inventory accuracy is not surprising in isolation — it is well established in the literature that event-driven updates outperform batch reconciliation for stock visibility. What is more interesting here is where the gains are concentrated. Veterinary supplements and prescription diets showed the largest improvements, at 21.4 and 19.7 percentage points respectively. These are exactly the products where a single missed unit matters most: each item is a significant share of total category stock, and stock discrepancies of even one or two units can trigger unnecessary replenishment orders or, worse, mask a genuine stockout. The results suggest that the ROI of event-driven inventory infrastructure is highest for low-volume, high-specialization product categories — a pattern that maps directly onto the core stock-in-trade of independent pet retailers.
The findings also confirm that the event-driven architecture benefits described in Section 2 are not limited to large enterprise deployments. A five-branch independent retailer running on commodity cloud infrastructure can achieve inventory accuracy levels comparable to those reported for major omnichannel chains, provided the update mechanism is genuinely event-driven rather than batch-scheduled. That is an important practical takeaway for the stores this framework is designed to serve.

### 8.2. Stockout Reduction and Its Commercial Implications
An 83.1% reduction in stockout rate is the result that matters most commercially. Pet retail is a loyalty-driven category: a customer who cannot find the specific prescription diet their dog needs today will not wait — they will order from Supertails, Amazon, or Zepto within minutes. Unlike a supermarket shopper who substitutes one brand for another, pet owners buying veterinary diets or specialized supplements often cannot substitute at all. Every stockout in those categories is effectively a lost customer relationship, not just a lost sale. By combining accurate real-time inventory visibility with forward-looking LSTM forecasts and cooperative multi-branch replenishment, PawKart attacks the stockout problem from three angles simultaneously, which explains why the reduction is as large as it is.
The remaining 2.1% stockout rate reflects the irreducible tail of unpredictable demand spikes — emergency purchases, sudden dietary changes on veterinary advice, rapid seasonal lifts — that occur faster than any forecasting or replenishment system can respond to given normal supplier lead times. Closing that gap entirely would require a different class of solution, such as micro-fulfillment agreements with nearby veterinary clinics or a small emergency stock reserve held off the main shelving system. We have flagged this in the future scope.

### 8.3. Reading the Forecasting Numbers Honestly
An 8.1% overall MAPE is a solid result, but it deserves an honest reading. The aggregate number is pulled down by the large number of stable, recurring SKUs — standard dry food, treats, accessories — where demand is genuinely predictable from sales history and the LSTM has no trouble achieving sub-5% errors. The 15.2% MAPE for high-volatility products is more sobering, and it reflects a real limitation: history-based models cannot forecast demand that is driven by events outside the transaction record. A vet recommending a new prescription diet, or a sudden flea outbreak following the first monsoon rains, will not appear in the input features until after the associated sales spike has already started. No amount of LSTM capacity will overcome that information gap.
The comparison against ARIMA (14.2% MAPE) and the moving-average baseline (19.4% MAPE) confirms that the deep learning approach is genuinely outperforming classical alternatives, not just trading complexity for equivalent accuracy. The LSTM's advantage is most pronounced on medium-volatility products with non-linear seasonal patterns, where ARIMA's linearity assumptions start to break down. For low-volatility products, all three approaches perform similarly, which suggests that for those SKUs a simpler model could be substituted without meaningful accuracy loss if computational resources were constrained.

### 8.4. Sub-30-Minute Delivery: What We Actually Showed
The 78.4% sub-30-minute rate for express-tier orders is worth contextualizing carefully. The primary bottleneck in the baseline was not the delivery itself — a competent Dunzo driver can cover 3 km in 12 minutes in Bengaluru traffic — it was the 18.4-minute average pre-dispatch latency caused by manual stock checking, handwritten pick list preparation, and phone-based delivery partner coordination. PawKart reduced that pre-dispatch time to 4.7 minutes by automating all three steps. The delivery leg time stayed roughly constant. In other words, the path to quick-commerce for an independent pet store does not require a new warehouse, a fleet of bikes, or a dark store — it requires eliminating the administrative dead time between order confirmation and handing a parcel to a driver.
The 21.6% of express orders that missed the 30-minute window were almost all multi-SKU orders where at least one item was out of stock at the nearest branch and required a lateral transfer decision or a partial fulfilment conversation with the customer. The current pipeline does not handle partial fulfilment — it either fulfils everything or nothing. Fixing that is the most impactful near-term enhancement we can make, and we have scoped it in Section 10.

### 8.5. Broader Significance
The results taken together make a case that is more general than PawKart itself. The operational gap between independent specialty retailers and quick-commerce platforms is real, but it is not structural — it is technological. Closing it does not require the capital investment of an enterprise digital transformation; it requires a purposefully designed, modular system that wraps the right combination of event streaming, machine learning, and workflow automation around the existing operations of a small store. PawKart demonstrates that such a system is buildable with open-source tooling and deployable on commodity infrastructure at a cost that is accessible to a store earning margins typical of the Indian pet retail market.
For the omnichannel retail research community, this is a meaningful extension of the existing literature. Most theoretical and empirical work on real-time inventory and quick-commerce fulfilment has been conducted at enterprise scale, which has the practical consequence that its prescriptions are not actionable for small retailers. By grounding the same architectural principles in a system designed from the outset for resource-constrained independent operators, we hope to shift some of that research attention toward the SME specialty retail segment.

### 8.6. Limitations We Want to Be Transparent About
Three limitations are worth flagging directly. First, the entire evaluation is simulation-based. We designed the synthetic dataset carefully, but it is still synthetic — it cannot reproduce the full messiness of real supplier relationships, human data-entry errors, physical RFID misreads, or the idiosyncratic demand patterns of a specific store's actual customer base. A live field deployment is the only way to know whether the performance numbers hold up. Second, the RFID sensing layer described in the methodology was simulated; we did not physically deploy sensors on shelves. Hardware reliability, read accuracy under real shelf conditions, and the latency introduced by actual scanning infrastructure are all unknowns at this stage. Third, the MARL environment used a symmetric five-branch topology. Real store networks are messier — branches have different footprints, different supplier relationships, and different demand profiles, and the RL agents may need retraining or reward function adjustments to handle that heterogeneity well.

## 9. Conclusion

PawKart set out to answer a practical question: can an independent pet store, operating with the resources typical of a small specialty retailer in Bengaluru, achieve the inventory accuracy and order fulfilment speed that quick-commerce platforms now treat as table stakes? Our answer, based on the system we built and the simulation evaluation we ran, is yes — but only with the right combination of real-time event-driven architecture, predictive machine learning, and automated workflow coordination working together as a unified system. None of the three components alone is sufficient.
The headline results — 97.3% inventory accuracy, 83.1% stockout reduction, 8.1% demand forecasting MAPE, and sub-30-minute fulfilment for 78.4% of express orders — represent a genuine and substantial improvement over the manual baseline, and they are consistent with what the enterprise retail literature suggests is achievable with event-driven and ML-powered infrastructure. The fact that we achieved them using entirely open-source tooling, on a stack that can realistically be operated by a two-person technical team, is the contribution we are most satisfied with.
Theoretically, this work extends the omnichannel inventory literature into territory it has not adequately covered: the independent specialty retailer. Most of the frameworks, models, and prescriptions in that literature are calibrated to the capabilities and resource profiles of large chains. The architecture presented here — seven decoupled layers, each replaceable and independently scalable — offers a design template that can be adapted to other small specialty retail contexts beyond pet stores: independent pharmacies, organic food boutiques, baby product retailers. The demand forecasting and replenishment optimization components are domain-agnostic; only the feature engineering and the product taxonomy need to change.
We are under no illusion that simulation results alone are sufficient to validate a production system. The next and most important step is getting PawKart into a real store and running it against real transactions, real suppliers, and real customers. That field deployment is what Section 10 describes as our immediate priority.

## 10. Future Scope
The work presented here is a proof of concept, and there are several directions we want to pursue in the next phase of research and development.
Live Field Deployment. Everything we have measured so far is against synthetic data, and synthetic data has limits. The most pressing next step is deploying PawKart in one or two real independent pet stores in Bengaluru and running a longitudinal study over at least six months. A live deployment will surface the hardware integration issues we have not yet encountered (RFID read reliability on crowded shelves, network connectivity in older store premises), the demand patterns our synthetic generator did not reproduce, and the usability problems that only show up when a store owner who has never touched a machine learning dashboard tries to use it on a busy Saturday morning.

External Signals for High-Volatility SKUs. The 15.2% MAPE for high-volatility products is the forecasting result we are least satisfied with. The core issue is that the drivers of demand spikes for prescription diets and emergency treatments exist outside the transaction record. We plan to explore integrating three categories of external signal into the feature set: hyperlocal weather data (which correlates with flea and tick treatment demand across monsoon transitions), aggregated veterinary consultation volume from partner clinics in the catchment area (which leads prescription diet demand by 2–4 days), and social media and search trend signals for novel product categories. We expect these additions to bring high-volatility MAPE below 10%.

Physical RFID Sensing Validation. The current system simulates RFID-based sensing. We need to implement and test the full physical pipeline: commercial UHF RFID tags applied to shelf facings, fixed readers mounted on shelf frames, and the middleware that converts raw antenna reads into clean InventoryEvent records. The interesting research questions here are around read accuracy in environments with dense metallic packaging (pet food tins and foil pouches are notoriously problematic for RFID), and whether sensor misreads introduce systematic biases into the inventory accuracy metric.

Partial Fulfilment and Order Splitting. The 21.6% of express orders that missed the 30-minute window is largely a partial-fulfilment problem. We want to build a workflow that, when a multi-SKU order cannot be fully assembled at the nearest branch, automatically splits it: available items are dispatched immediately, and unavailable items are either sourced from a second branch (with a separate delivery leg) or offered as a follow-up delivery. Simulations suggest this alone would push the sub-30-minute rate above 90%.

Generalisation to Other Specialty Retail Verticals. The PawKart infrastructure is domain-agnostic below the feature engineering layer. We are interested in evaluating whether the same architecture, with adapted product taxonomies and demand features, can serve independent pharmacies and organic grocery boutiques — two other specialty retail contexts in Bengaluru where small operators face structurally similar quick-commerce pressure. If the architecture generalises well, we would like to produce a configurable template that any small specialty retailer could adapt without starting from scratch.

Scaling the MARL Policy. The cooperative PPO policy was tested at five branches. We have not yet examined how it behaves as the network grows to 20 or 50 stores, where the joint observation space becomes large and agent communication overhead starts to matter. Hierarchical RL approaches — where regional coordinator agents manage groups of store agents — are the obvious direction, and we plan to explore them alongside communication-efficient MARL algorithms that can reduce the information exchange needed to sustain cooperative behaviour at scale.

Mobile App for Store Associates. The current dashboard is browser-based and optimised for a desktop or tablet. Store associates doing shelf checks or receiving stock deliveries need a mobile-native experience: a quick barcode scan to confirm a receipt, a one-tap button to flag a damaged item, a notification when a priority pick-and-pack order needs to be assembled. A purpose-built companion app for store associates is the user-facing enhancement most likely to improve event stream completeness and therefore inventory accuracy in a real deployment.
